Which pages should you refresh first?

Ranking content decay across 30,000 pages, and what a leakage audit found hiding in the data.
Dawn Andrei Pamesa · August 2026 · Repository and notebooks

Abstract

Which pages should a content team review first, when there are more pages than review hours? This work ranks 30,000 pseudonymized content items across 32 clients, using a 90-day performance window from the FlyRank ML Internship starter release, against a label derived from the 30-day-over-previous-30-day impression change. A transparent hand rule was built first, then compared against logistic regression and a random forest under a client-grouped 5-fold split, with a leakage audit run on the final feature set. The random forest ranked declining pages at precision@1000 of 0.726 (sd 0.054) against a base rate of 0.542, ahead of the hand rule's 0.558 on all five held-out client folds; the audit also found that two shipped columns reconstruct the label exactly, and that a naive row-level split would have overstated the top-of-queue result by 22.7%. The output is a 1,000-page review queue with reason codes, intended to order human review of refresh candidates, and it is not evidence that refreshing any page recovers traffic.

1. The problem

A content team has 30,000 pages and enough review hours for maybe a thousand. Somebody has to decide which ones a human opens first. In practice that order gets set by whoever asked most recently, or by whichever client is loudest.

The cost of getting it wrong runs in both directions, and the two directions are not symmetric. Reviewing a page that was fine costs three to five hours of editorial time. Missing a page that is quietly decaying means it keeps decaying, and recovering visibility later is far more expensive than defending it now.

That base rate is the number worth holding on to. Just over half the pages in this corpus are declining, so picking pages at random already finds one about half the time. Every result below is quoted next to it, because a precision score without its base rate is not a result.

A scope change, stated up front

This project was declared in Week 1 as AI Referral and GEO Opportunity Scoring: finding pages with strong search demand but disproportionately low AI referral traffic. The modelled deliverable is narrower, and the reason belongs here rather than in a footnote.

The AI visibility gap is real and measurable. 16,726 pages (55.75%) have organic demand of at least 500 impressions, and 15,033 of those (89.88%) receive zero AI referral traffic. What does not exist is a label for it. Nothing in the release records whether an AI engine later cited a page, so the gap can be described and ranked by rule, but it cannot be learned, validated, or checked for leakage. Building a supervised model on it would have meant inventing a target.

The decay half of the same problem does have a label, so that is the half that carries the modelling. Reporting the missing label as a finding, rather than working around it, is the honest version of this project.

2. Data

The FlyRank ML Internship starter release: 30,000 pseudonymized content items, 44 columns, 32 clients, over a trailing 90-day performance window. Clients appear only as opaque identifiers and are used for fold assignment, never as a feature.

The label. A page is declining when its impressions over the last 30 days fell more than 20% against the previous 30 days.

A documentation discrepancy. The published FlyRank research paper defines this cut at 10%. The shipped data cuts at 20%. Reconstructing the label at a 10% threshold agrees with the shipped label on 93.3% of rows; at 20% it agrees on 100.0%. Anything built on the documented figure will not reproduce. Flagged here rather than silently corrected.

Excluded on purpose. trend_direction and trend_pct because the label is derived from them. provider_used and model_used as out of scope for a refresh decision. impressions_last_30d and impressions_prev_30d for the reason in section 4, which is the most interesting thing this project found.

3. Methodology

The question is "which first", which is a ranking question, so every scorer is judged on its predicted probability at precision@K rather than on hard labels. Accuracy appears nowhere in this work: a review queue is never consumed by thresholding at 0.5, it is consumed from the top down until the reviewer runs out of time.

The baseline came first

Before any model, a transparent hand rule scoring each page 0 to 100 from five stated conditions: stale and visible, thin and visible, page-one decay risk, low CTR while visible, and low engagement. Every point in the score traces to one sentence, so any row's position can be explained without a chart. The rule is scored on the identical test rows of each fold, so the comparison is a comparison.

The split is a correctness issue, not a preference

Folds are grouped by client, so no client appears in both train and test. That matters more than it usually does here: there are only 32 clients, one holds 12.3% of the corpus, and the per-client declining rate runs from 0.000 to 0.937 against a corpus rate of 0.542. Under a row-level shuffle the same client sits on both sides, and a model can score well by inferring which client a page belongs to and predicting that client's base rate. It would look like a content model and behave like a lookup table.

A time-aware split is not available: the starter release is a single undated snapshot. That design is recorded for the dated warehouse release rather than claimed here.

4. What the leakage audit found

Week 3 excluded the label-derived columns by name. That was correct and it was not enough.

The feature vector ships impressions_last_30d and impressions_prev_30d. The label is defined as the change between exactly those two windows. So they are not correlated with the label. They are the label, in unreduced form. The reconstruction is exact on 30,000 of 30,000 rows, with no residual.

This is the transferable lesson. The scan is worth running and it is not sufficient. What caught this was starting from the label's definition and asking which shipped columns appear in it: an audit of the data dictionary, not of the correlation table. Neither column is in the model, and the exclusion is now enforced by an assertion rather than by memory.

What the split was worth

Under the naive split the forest reaches precision@50 of 0.952. It is a real computation and a false claim. A shuffled-label null under the grouped design scores ROC-AUC 0.5016 (sd 0.0047), confirming the harness measures the label and not the folds.

What the model actually ranks on

The forest's top four features are all about observed visibility, not content shape: days with impressions (0.130), log impressions (0.130), average position (0.108), and content age (0.084), together roughly 45% of total importance. Word count sits at 0.046.

The most useful negative result in the project sits here. Days since last update carried the hand rule's single heaviest weight, 30 points, on the reasoning that a page nobody has touched in six months is the most urgent case. The forest ranks it 12th, at 0.023, below scroll rate. Two independent lines of evidence agree the staleness rule was a bad bet: it fired on 17 of 30,000 rows, and the model does not use it when it is free to. Recency of editing appears not to carry information about whether a page is declining, at least in this corpus.

The depth-2 tree makes the same point in one readable line:

days_with_impressions > 12  AND  content_age_days <= 373   →  at risk

Its entire learned rule is "a page that shows up consistently and is not yet very old is the one at risk". Nothing about word count, CTR, or edit recency.

The two scorers disagree, and measuring that is a result

When the hand rule's reason codes were attached to the forest's queue, they explained only 55.6% of it. The rule fires on thinness, staleness, and weak CTR. The forest ranks on sustained visibility and age, for which the rule has no reason code at all. That gap is not a bug in either scorer. It is the clearest single statement of what the model learned that the hand rule did not, and it is why the delivered queue carries model-derived reason codes in a separate column, labelled as such.

6. Limitations

These are the boundaries of the work, written before a reader has to find them.

  • This does not predict that refreshing a page recovers traffic. No refresh outcome exists anywhere in the data. The model ranks pages by whether impressions already fell, not by whether an edit would help. Every result is observational.
  • The corpus is survivors only. All 30,000 rows pass the active-content filter (impressions and sessions both above zero). The model has never seen a page that already died and cannot rank one. The same filter sits under several findings in the source research paper, which means neither that paper's survivorship question nor mine can be audited from inside this file.
  • 3,388 pages are structurally unrankable. They have no prior 30-day window, so under the label definition they cannot be declining, and all 3,388 are labelled not-declining by construction. They are excluded from the queue rather than ranked low, because ranking them would be an artefact of the label rather than a prediction.
  • Performance is markedly client-dependent. Fold metrics swing by up to 0.20 depending on which clients land in the test fold. There is no basis for claiming even generalisation to a new client, and the queue should be worked within a client rather than across clients.
  • The advantage at the top of the queue is directional, not consistent. The forest leads the hand rule at precision@1000 on five of five folds, but at precision@50 on only four of five. On the fifth fold the hand rule wins, 0.76 to 0.62.
  • The within-queue ordering is not explainable. The model-derived reason codes fire on the whole queue, so they explain the population rather than why row 12 sits above row 400.
  • No sealed evaluation is claimed. The contract defines a sealed test month; the starter release is undated, so the design is recorded and not executed.

7. Recommendations

The shipped output is a 1,000-page queue drawn from 26,612 eligible pages, scored out of fold so that every page is ranked by a model that never saw its client. Working it top down surfaces 778 declining pages against roughly 542 expected from an arbitrary order: about 236 fewer wasted reviews per thousand.

Four checks before any page is edited

  1. Is the decline real, or is it the measurement? The label is one 30-day window against the previous one. A seasonal dip, a SERP feature change, or a tracking gap all read as declining.
  2. Does the reason code match what you see? If the source column says model, the hand rule found nothing wrong with the page. Read that as "the model noticed a pattern", not as a diagnosis.
  3. Is a refresh the right instrument? A page can decline because the topic died, because a competitor published something better, or because another page cannibalised it. Only one of those is fixed by refreshing, and the model has no feature for any of them.
  4. Would you defend this edit to the client? "It was ranked 12th" is not a justification.

Never automated, at any confidence score

Publishing an edit. Deleting, de-indexing, or redirecting a page. Client-facing performance promises. Budget allocation between clients. Bulk machine regeneration of flagged pages.

The confidence column is not a calibrated probability. The bands are tertiles of this queue's own score distribution, so "high" means high relative to the other 999 rows in front of you, and nothing more. The model's probabilities were never calibrated and no calibration is claimed.

Monitoring

Six triggers ship with the queue, each anchored to a measured value rather than a round number, using the 0.054 fold spread as the scale of ordinary variation. A trigger inside that band is a false alarm generator. The two that matter most: queue precision falling below 0.670, which means retrain; and any change to the 20% label threshold, the window, or the metric, which means stop and rebuild, because every number in this paper was computed against that cut.
